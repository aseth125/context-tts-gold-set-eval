How a sentence was said — annotator brief

Thank you for taking this on. Please read this once before you start. It takes about five minutes and the task will make much more sense afterwards.

1. What this is about

When people talk, the same written sentence can be said in completely different ways depending on what came before it.

"Right, I'll give it a go."

Read that on its own and you cannot tell much. But if the person had just been handed a recommendation they had never heard of, they are taking something new on board. If they had already been told twice and want the subject dropped, they are just being polite. Same words, different thing happening.

We are building a test set for speech systems. For that we need to know, for each recording: does the conversation before it actually tell you how the sentence should be said, or could anyone have guessed it from the words alone?

You are the people who answer that. You do not need any training in speech or linguistics. If anything in the task needs specialist knowledge, that is a fault in our design, not in you — please tell us.

2. What you will hear

Everything is a real recording of two real people having a real conversation. Nothing is computer-generated, and nothing is being judged for quality. Some recordings are a bit messy — people talk over each other, laugh, trail off. That is normal and expected.

For each item you get:

what it is how long
The conversation so far written out, and as audio about 1.5 minutes (shortest 28 s, longest 2 minutes)
The sentence one line, said next by one of the two speakers 3 to 20 words
The recording of that sentence the real person saying it about 4 seconds (1.5 s to 9 s)

The run-up audio stops the instant the sentence begins, so you never hear the sentence by accident before you are meant to.

3. The task: three questions, in this order

Each item asks you the same question three times, and each time you are given one more thing to go on. The page gives you them one at a time and will not let you skip ahead.

Task 1 — Just the sentence

You see only the written sentence. You pick, from five short descriptions, how you think it was said.

You are meant to be unsure here. You have nothing to go on. Make a quick guess — a few seconds — and move on. A careful guess and a wild guess count exactly the same to us.

Task 2 — With the conversation

Now you can read the conversation and listen to the run-up. You still do not hear the sentence itself. Pick again from the same five.

This is the most important one. The question is: now that I know how this conversation got here, how should that sentence be said?

Task 3 — With the recording

Now you hear the person actually say it. Pick again, for what they really do.

At each task, keep your previous answer or change it — both are genuinely useful. Your earlier answers stay on screen so you do not have to remember them.

4. Why three times

This is the whole point, so it is worth a minute.

  • If people get it right at Task 1, the words carried the meaning and the conversation was not needed. That item is no use to us.
  • If people only get it right at Task 2, the conversation supplied it. That is exactly the kind of item we want.
  • If Task 3 disagrees with Task 2, the speaker did something the conversation did not lead you to expect.

Nobody has to judge any of that. It falls out of your three answers on its own.

So please do not try to be clever. Two things in particular:

  • Do not try to be right at Task 1. If you reason hard and get it right, you make a genuinely context-dependent item look like it was not.
  • Do not guess the recording at Task 2. Answer what the conversation points to, not what you suspect the person probably did.

5. How sure are you?

After each pick, mark Low, Medium or High. It is part of the answer, not an extra, and the page will not move on until you give it.

Expect Low at Task 1 — you are guessing from a bare sentence. If Task 2 is High where Task 1 was Low, that is the conversation doing its job, and it is one of the most useful things you can tell us.

Marking Low when you are unsure costs you nothing and makes your confident answers worth more. Please use it honestly.

6. "None of these fit"

The last choice on every task. Use it freely:

  • at Task 1, if the sentence gives you nothing at all — not "I am torn between two", but "none of these is a reading of this sentence";
  • at Task 2, if the conversation genuinely does not point at any of them;
  • at Task 3, if what you heard is none of the five.

It is a real answer and it is recorded as one. Forcing a pick you do not believe is worse than useless — a random wrong answer at Task 1 followed by a right one at Task 2 looks exactly like a line that needed its conversation, which is the thing we are trying to count. You would be creating a false result, not avoiding one.

7. If something is broken

Tick flag and say what is wrong in the comment box. Please flag:

  • a clip that is cut off, silent, or far too quiet
  • the wrong person speaking
  • speech so overlapped you cannot make out the line
  • a transcript that does not match what you hear
  • anything that just seems wrong

These are useful findings and they get acted on. You are not being a nuisance.

8. Scale: this batch and the full job

This batch is a pilot of 13 items. The full job is around 500 items.

The pilot exists to catch problems while they are cheap to fix. Specifically we want to know, before we scale:

  • are any of the five choices too close together to tell apart?
  • is anything in this brief unclear or misleading?
  • how long does an item really take you?
  • is the audio quality workable?

So on this batch, please over-report. If an option is confusing, if two of them seem the same, if a clip is hard to hear, if a question does not make sense — write it in the comment box or tell us directly. On the pilot that feedback is worth more to us than the answers themselves, because every problem you find here is one that would otherwise be repeated 500 times.

Pacing for the full job: at 4 to 5 minutes an item, 500 items is roughly 33 to 42 hours per pass. About 13 hours of that is audio you simply have to sit through — the run-up averages a minute and a half — so a little over a third of the time is listening and the rest is reading and deciding.

That is a comfortable pace rather than a tight one, and it is deliberate. Please use it to listen to the run-up properly rather than reading only the transcript: how something was said in the conversation carries as much of the setup as the words do, and Task 2 does not work without it.

We plan 3 annotators on every item, so the full job is about 100 to 125 hours of work in total, best split across several people.

10. The three categories, in plain terms

(Every example below is invented. None of them is one of the real items, so nothing here gives away an answer.)

The five choices you pick between are different on each page.

Intent — what the speaker is doing. Things like Strongly agreeing, Telling them they got it wrong, Stating a fact, Checking they heard it right, Making a point, not expecting an answer.

Note that several of these can look identical on paper. "Weren't you there yesterday?" could be someone genuinely asking, someone checking they heard right, or someone making a point. Only the conversation separates them. That is deliberate — it is the whole experiment.

Emotion — how the speaker feels. Things like Amused and laughing, Grossed out, Irritated and fed up, Excited and hopeful.

Emphasis — which word they lean on. Here the choices describe which words are hit hard and which are thrown away, for example:

Stress 'Tuesday'. Throw away 'the meeting' — say it quick and quiet.

This one is about which word carries the weight. When something has already been mentioned it usually gets rushed past; when something is new it gets the weight. Say the options out loud to yourself if it helps — most people find that the quickest way to hear the difference.

11. Common questions

"I don't know what the difference between two of the options is." Then they are too close together and that is our mistake. Pick either, mark Low, and please say so in the comment box.

"I picked the same answer all three times. Is that wrong?" No. It is a perfectly good result — it tells us the sentence carried its own meaning. We expect a fair number of these.

"I changed my mind at every task." Also fine, and also informative.

"The recording sounds nothing like what the conversation suggested." Say so at Task 3 — pick what you actually hear, or "None of these fit". That mismatch is a real finding and we want to know about it.

"Can I go back and change an earlier task?" Task 1 locks once you answer it, because the measurement depends on it being your honest first guess before you knew anything. Later tasks stay open until you have heard the recording.

"Am I being tested?" No. There is no score for you, and no right answer we are checking you against. We are measuring the recordings, not you. An honest "Low confidence, none of these" is worth far more to us than a confident guess.

12. The one thing to remember

Answer each task with only what that task has given you. Do not reach ahead to what you suspect, and do not reach back to defend an answer you have already given. Three honest answers, each from what was in front of you at the time, is exactly what we need.

Thank you — and please do send questions. This is a pilot: anything confusing here is far cheaper to fix now than after 500 items have been done the wrong way, so tell us early and tell us bluntly.
